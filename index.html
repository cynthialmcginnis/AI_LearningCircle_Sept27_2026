    Part 1

    1

    Where are you with GenAI?

    How you use GenAI now, for your own work and for your students. What changed since it arrived, and why.

    13 minutes · discussion

    Part 1 · Where are you with GenAI?

    Flip a card. Then talk.

    Each card starts with a finding from this year's EDUCAUSE research. The back asks how it looks in your own work.

    Give an example from your course, not an opinion.

    13 minutes. Flip one card at a time. Ask a named person to answer first, then open it up. Card 1 is productivity, card 2 is student activities, card 3 pulls it together. Keep a list of anything people say they need. It feeds the USM Fellows needs assessment.

    Part 2

    2

    AI Use Disclosure Form

    Two drafts of a form students attach to assignments. You decide what to keep and how it should be written.

    10 minutes · decide together

    Part 2 · AI Use Disclosure Form

    Two drafts. Which one would your students actually fill out?

    1. Draft A or Draft B? Or a mix?
    2. What would you cut?
    3. What would you reword?
    4. Can a student honestly estimate what percentage came from AI?
    5. Should "I did not use GenAI" be an option on every assignment?
    10 minutes. Give 60 seconds of silent reading. Then take the questions in order. Write decisions on your own copy. Offer both files for download so faculty can mark them up and send them back.

    Draft A: AI Use Disclosure

    Short. Attach to any assignment where you used AI assistance.

    1. Name, date, course, assignment
    2. AI tool used
    3. How I used AI on this assignment
    4. Prompts I entered into the AI
    5. How I verified or modified the AI output
    6. Approximate percentage of work that came from AI
    7. Student signature

    Signature confirms the disclosure is accurate and all AI content was reviewed, verified, and cited.

    Download Draft A

    Draft B: AI Use Disclosure Form

    Detailed. Submit with any assignment where GenAI was used, per the instructor's policy.

    1. Name, course and section, instructor, assignment, date
    2. Did you use GenAI? Yes or No (No skips to signature)
    3. Which tools, with version if known
    4. How did you use it? Checklist: brainstorming, research, first draft, revising, grammar or citations, debugging, other. Plus a description.
    5. What did you change or verify? What is your own work?
    6. Attestation and signature

    Attestation adds: I take responsibility for all content, and my use follows my instructor's stated policy.

    Download Draft B

    AI use disclosure by faculty

    We ask students to disclose. Should we? If so, what would we say, and where?

    8 minutes · discussion

    Part 3 · AI use disclosure by faculty

    What should we tell students about our own GenAI use?

    1. Do you disclose now? Where?
    2. Which version fits your course?
    3. What counts? A grammar check? Slide design? Quiz questions?
    4. Does disclosing change how students treat the rule we give them?
    8 minutes. Read the three versions aloud. Ask: "Which would you put in your syllabus tomorrow?" Then question 4. It connects back to Part 2.
    Short: one syllabus line

    I use GenAI tools to help draft some course materials. I review, edit, and take responsibility for everything I post.

    Specific: what and how

    In this course I used GenAI to draft practice problems, outline slides, and check my wording. I checked every problem and answer myself. GenAI does not grade your work.

    Item by item: a label on each material

    "Practice set drafted with Claude, reviewed and edited by the instructor." A short note on each document, slide deck, or activity where GenAI played a part.

    These are starting drafts for discussion, not UMGC policy.

    Part 4

    4

    Assignment Design Guidelines

    Decide the GenAI policy for each assignment first. Then design the assignment around it.

    8 minutes · review and discuss

    Part 4 · Assignment Design Guidelines

    Every assignment gets one of three policies.

    1. Pick one assignment you teach. Which policy fits it, and why?
    2. The template models the "integrate" policy. What would the guidelines say for "prohibit"?
    3. What would you need to see to reuse a colleague's assignment?

    Policy options adapted from Cornell University's 2023 guidelines on generative AI in education.

    8 minutes. Go straight to question 1 and use "Who's next?" for the first answer. The template download is for later. Don't walk through it.

    Prohibit

    When GenAI would replace the foundational skill students need for later courses and careers. Tell students why the process matters.

    Allow with attribution

    When GenAI can help, but you need to know how it was used. Students disclose and take responsibility for accuracy.

    Integrate

    When GenAI lets students reach higher-level work: analysis, creativity, evaluation. Its use is part of the assignment.

    The template has two parts

    Part I, instructor planning: motivation, problem statement, learning objectives, GenAI integration strategy, and expected outcomes.

    Part II, student-facing assignment: description, why it matters, components, GenAI documentation requirements, deliverables, grading, and timeline.

    Download the template

    Part 5

    5

    Lesson Plan Builder and Assessor

    A Hugging Face app that checks a PowerPoint for backward design and interactivity, then builds a lesson plan.

    7 minutes · demo

    Part 5 · Lesson Plan Builder and Assessor

    Upload a deck. Get a report and a lesson plan.

    It checks two things. Backward design: do your outcomes, activities, and assessment line up? Interactivity: where do students do something instead of watch?

    Open the app

    7 minutes. Run it live on one of your own decks. Ask the room to predict first: "Where will it say my deck is weakest?" Then show the lesson plan and point to one thing you would change.

    How to use it

    1. Open the app. If it says it is sleeping or starting, wait a minute.
    2. Upload a PowerPoint from one of your lessons.
    3. Read the backward design check. Look for outcomes with no activity or assessment behind them.
    4. Read the interactivity check. Look for long stretches where students only watch.
    5. Build the lesson plan.
    6. Edit the plan. You know your students. The app doesn't.

    Before you upload

    Remove student names, grades, and anything else about students from the deck.

    Try it this week

    Run one deck through it before November 30. Bring the report. We'll compare what it caught.

    Part 6

    6

    Vibe coding

    How to write a prompt that gets you a working HTML and JavaScript tool, and how to share it with students.

    12 minutes · demo · November is hands-on

    Part 6 · Vibe coding

    One prompt made this tool.

    You don't write code. You describe the tool: who it's for, what students do, what they see, and the rules it must follow. GenAI writes the HTML and JavaScript.

    1

    Demo (6 min). Let someone call out the number of dice. Then point to the prompt on the right, part by part: purpose, what students do, requirements, output. The last two lines are what make it work in Copilot.
    Write a complete web page in HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. Put all of it in one code block so I can copy it into a single file named dice.html.

The page is a teaching tool for an introductory statistics course. It shows students the central limit theorem.

What the student does and sees:
- A slider to choose how many dice to roll at once, from 1 to 12.
- A button labeled "Roll 1,000 times."
- A bar chart showing how often each average came up.
- One sentence under the chart explaining what the shape means.

Requirements:
- No external libraries. It must work without an internet connection.
- One short instruction line at the top of the page.
- Large, readable text, and a label on every control.
- Works on a phone and a laptop.

Write the full code. Do not shorten it, skip parts, or describe it instead of writing it.

    Change it for your course

    Keep the first and last paragraphs. Rewrite the middle: the course, the idea, what students do, and what they see.

    Part 6 · Vibe coding

    When Copilot doesn't cooperate

    • It explains instead of writing code. Reply: "Write the complete code in one code block."
    • The code stops partway. Reply: "Continue exactly where you stopped." Paste the two parts together.
    • It offers to make a document or Page. Reply: "No. Show the code here in the chat."
    • It keeps cutting the code short. Ask for a smaller tool first: one idea, one screen. Or try Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini.

    Save it as a file

    Windows: paste into Notepad. Save As, name it dice.html, and set "Save as type" to All files.

    Mac: paste into TextEdit. Choose Format, then Make Plain Text. Save it as dice.html.

    Double-click the file. It opens in your browser.

    Sharing (6 min). Ask: "Where would your students actually open this?" The answer decides which option they use.

    Share it with students

    Option 1: upload the file to your course

    Add the .html file to your course classroom like any other file. Open it in student view first. Some systems show it in the browser, others download it.

    Option 2: put it online with GitHub Pages

    1. Create a free GitHub account and a new public repository.
    2. Upload the file and name it index.html.
    3. Open Settings, then Pages. Under Source, choose "Deploy from a branch," pick main, and save.
    4. Wait a minute or two. GitHub shows your link. Post the link in your course.

    Option 3: send the file

    Email or post the file. Students double-click to open it. No account needed.

    Before students see it

    Test it on a phone. Try a strange input. Check the content yourself. Working code can still teach the wrong idea.
